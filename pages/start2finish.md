Framing a House: Start to Finish
1. Foundation Verification & Layout
Before any lumber is cut, the foundation (slab, crawl space, or basement wall) is checked for level and square (diagonals measured and compared). The building footprint is transferred to the foundation surface with chalk lines, establishing the layout for everything above.

2. Sill Plates & Floor System
Sill plates (pressure-treated 2×6 or 2×8) are set on the foundation over a sill seal gasket and fastened with anchor bolts (typically ½" diameter, spaced 4–6 ft apart).
Rim joists (header joists) are attached at right angles to the sill plates, forming the perimeter band.
Floor joists (2×8, 2×10, or 2×12) are laid perpendicular to the rim joists at 16" or 24" OC, nailed to the rim on each end.
Blocking and bridging are added between joists for lateral stability.
Subfloor (¾" plywood or OSB) is screwed to the joists with staggered panel joints, creating a solid platform.
3. Wall Framing
Walls are pre-built flat on the subfloor in sections (typically 8–16 ft panels), then raised:

Bottom plate is laid, studs marked at 16" OC.
Studs are nailed to the bottom plate. 
Rough openings are framed for doors and windows: king studs (full height), jack/trimmer studs (shorter, supporting the header), header (laminated 2× or 3× lumber), and cripple studs (short studs above header and below sill).
Top plates — a double top plate (two 2×4s or 2×6s stacked) locks adjacent wall panels together at corners and T-intersections.
The completed panel is tilted upright, braced with temporary diagonal struts, and toenailed/screwed to the subfloor.
Exterior walls go first (longest walls first to establish corners), then interior partition walls.
4. Sheathing & Bracing
Exterior wall sheathing (⅝" or ¾" plywood/OSB) is nailed to the studs, locking the wall panel into a rigid shear box.
Cross-bracing or diagonal bracing is added to interior walls and any un-sheathed surfaces.
Temporary bracing is removed only after the structure is fully locked in.
5. Second-Floor Platform (if applicable)
Second-floor joists are set on the top plates of the first-floor walls.
A second subfloor is installed, creating the platform for the upper story.
The process repeats: walls are framed flat, raised, and sheathed.
6. Roof Framing
Two common approaches:

Option A – Prefab Trusses:

Engineered roof trusses are delivered and set with a crane or sticks, spaced 24" OC.
Collar ties, lookouts (for eaves/fascia), and ridge boarding are added. 
Knee walls or attic framing is added for dormers or gable ends.
Option B – Stick Rafters:

A ridge board is set at the peak. 
Rafters (2×6 or 2×8) are cut with a bird's mouth notch and set in pairs, nailed to the top plate and ridge board.
Rafter ties (or ceiling joists) are nailed horizontally between the bottom of opposing rafters to resist outward wall thrust.
Purlins and collar ties are added as needed for span and stability.
7. Roof Sheathing & Drying-In
Roof sheathing (⅝" or ¾" plywood/OSB) is nailed to the rafters/trusses with staggered joints.
Fascia boards cap the rafter ends at the eaves.
Drip edge, ice & water shield, and synthetic underlayment are applied.
The structure is now "dried in" — ready for the building envelope (house wrap, windows, doors) and mechanical rough-ins.
Typical timeline: For a single-family home, the entire framing phase (sill plate through roof sheathing) takes 3–7 days with a crew of 4–6 framers, depending on complexity. Inspections (framing, sheathing, and sometimes a "dry-in" check) are required at key milestones before proceeding to the next trade
Sill Plate Materials
Pressure-treated lumber is the standard and what most codes require for wood in contact with concrete.  Key details:

Material	Pros	Cons
PT Southern Yellow Pine (most common)	Cheap, widely available, decay/insect resistant	ACQ/CA-B treatment corrodes standard steel — must use hot-dip galvanized or stainless fasteners
PT Douglas Fir / Hem-Fir	Stronger than SYP, available in PNW	Same corrosion caveat; sometimes incised (reduces strength slightly)
Borate-treated lumber (e.g., "blue wood")	Non-toxic, no fastener corrosion, termite resistance	Not for repeated wetting; code acceptance varies by jurisdiction
Western Red Cedar	Naturally decay-resistant, no chemicals, straight and workable	Expensive; must be code-approved as a decay-resistant alternative
Metal (steel or aluminum)	Indestructible, no moisture issues	Expensive, thermal bridging, limited availability
Engineered wood (glulam, LVL)	High strength-to-weight	Still needs treatment or a capillary break if touching concrete

Code requirement (IRC R317.1): Wood in contact with masonry or concrete must be pressure-treated (UC4A minimum) or naturally decay-resistant. An EPDM sill gasket or foam sealer between the plate and foundation is a strong complement (capillary break + air seal) but does not replace the treatment requirement in most jurisdictions.

Size: Typically 2×6 or 2×8 PT, bolted to the foundation with ½" anchor bolts spaced 4–6 ft apart (closer in seismic zones).

Calculating Floor Joist Spacing
Spacing and span are inversely related — closer spacing allows longer spans (or lighter joists). The relationship:

w (line load on each joist) = total load (psf) × spacing (ft)

Example: 50 psf total load (40 live + 10 dead):

Spacing	Line load (lb/ft)
12" OC (1.0 ft)	50
16" OC (1.33 ft)	66.7
19.2" OC (1.6 ft)	80
24" OC (2.0 ft)	100

To find the maximum span at a given spacing, use the deflection formula (which governs for most residential floors):

L_max = ∛( 384 × E × I / (5 × 360 × w_live) )

Where:

E = modulus of elasticity (species/grade specific)
I = moment of inertia = b × d³ / 12 (for a 2×10: 1.5 × 9.25³/12 = 103.7 in⁴)
w_live = live load × spacing (lb/ft)
360 = L/360 deflection limit (IRC for floors) 
Practical shortcut: The IRC span tables (Table R502.3(1)) already encode this for standard species/grades at 12", 16", 19.2", and 24" OC. For most residential work, 16" OC is the default; 24" OC is used when the joist size is adequate (e.g., 2×12s or LVL) to save material; 12" OC is used for longer spans or lighter joists. 

Rule of thumb: Going from 16" to 24" OC increases the line load by 50%, so the maximum span drops by roughly 30–35%.

Purpose of Blocking
Blocking (short pieces of lumber fitted between adjacent framing members) serves several distinct functions:

Function	Where	Why
Lateral restraint	Between floor joists, between studs	Prevents joists from rotating/twisting under load (which reduces their effective section modulus and causes premature deflection). Keeps studs from buckling in compression.
Load transfer	At bearing points, under wall lines	Transfers concentrated loads (e.g., a wall bearing on 2–3 joists) to adjacent joists, distributing the weight and reducing localized deflection. 
Nailing surface	Behind future fixtures (grab bars, cabinets, mirrors)	Provides a solid backing for screws/nails where a stud or joist isn't present. 
Fire blocking	At floor/ceiling intersections, within walls	Required by IRC to slow fire and smoke spread through concealed cavities (typically at each floor level and at mid-height in walls over 10 ft).
Subfloor/finish support	Under subfloor panel edges, at stair openings	Prevents subfloor from spanning unsupported between joists; stiffens the floor assembly.
Seismic/bracing	At shear wall ends, around openings	Helps lock the framing into a rigid box that resists racking.

Code note (IRC R502.7): Lateral support (blocking, bridging, or attached subfloor) is required for joists with a depth-to-width ratio greater than 10:1 (e.g., 2×10 and deeper) unless the subfloor is ¾" or thicker and properly fastened. For I-joists, solid blocking is required at bearing points and at intervals per the manufacturer's span tables.
